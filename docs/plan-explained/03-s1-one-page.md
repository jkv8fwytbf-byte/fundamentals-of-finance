# Section 1: What we are building, in one page

**In short.** The first section of the plan describes the whole system in seven parts (plan section 1). The goal is a machine that does what you used to do by hand in Damodaran's spreadsheet, for many companies at once, in India and the US, private to you, with nothing on your Mac except an editor (plan section 1).

- **The calculator.** A program that takes a company's numbers and Damodaran's reference tables and computes value per share the way his spreadsheet does (plan section 1). Think of a pocket calculator checked against an answer key. The golden test is that answer key: the spreadsheet ships with a worked example, Almarai, a Saudi food company, valued at 7.187840270062114 per share, and the program must reproduce that number to six decimals before it may value anything else (plan section 1). "Exact" covers only the arithmetic, including how an industry beta (how much a stock swings with the market) is adjusted for the company's debt; it says nothing about whether the inputs are right (plan section 1). So every valuation will also produce a range from Monte Carlo draws (many reruns with varied inputs) and a sensitivity table (a grid showing how the value moves when one input at a time is changed) (plan section 1).

- **The database in the cloud (Neon).** Neon is a hosted database, a filing cabinet on the internet (plan section 1). Every number will carry three labels: source, license class and vintage (plan section 1). License class means whether you may show the number to other people (plan section 1). Vintage means which snapshot of Damodaran's data the number belongs to; he updates his tables every January, with quarterly and monthly refreshes of two of them, so one year holds several versions of the risk-free rate and the risk premium that must never be mixed (plan section 1). The database will refuse a number that is missing any label (plan section 1).

- **The librarian in the cloud (Pinecone and Voyage).** His 609 blog posts, lecture packets, book, datasets and transcripts will be cut into about 60,000 passages (plan section 1). Each passage becomes a vector, a list of numbers that captures its meaning, so a question can find the right passages, and answers cite them (plan section 1). Picture a librarian who hands you the exact page, not the whole book.

- **The memo writer.** For each company an AI model will read the whole latest filing in one go, up to about 1.3 million tokens (a token is a small chunk of text), plus the matching Damodaran passages (plan section 1). It will write a "story to numbers" memo in his own template, proposing each calculator input with a citation to a filing page or a Damodaran passage (plan section 1). Think of a junior analyst who must footnote every claim. The 3P test sorts each claim as possible, plausible or probable; only probable claims may set a base-case input, plausible ones set a scenario, and possible ones are recorded at zero (plan section 1). Reverse DCF asks what growth or margin the current share price already requires (plan section 1). The memo must also name the company's uncertainty type from his "Dark Side of Valuation", such as young, distressed, cyclical, financial-service, complex holding or emerging-market, and write down the failure probability and the expected range (plan section 1). You review, edit and approve; only then does the calculator run (plan section 1). A second, opposing memo per company is mandatory, the "Gurley field", named after the Uber episode where he re-ran his model on Bill Gurley's story (plan section 1).

- **Robots that run at night (GitHub Actions).** GitHub Actions is a scheduler that runs small jobs on rented computers, like a night watchman doing rounds (plan section 1). The jobs will pull new filings from SEC EDGAR, the free public archive of US filings, and later from EODHD, a paid service, for India (plan section 1). They will re-value your watchlist, check for a new Damodaran risk-premium file, pull licensed headlines, and write it all to the database (plan section 1). "Nightly, never intraday" means once a night is the right pace, and nothing here reacts to prices during the trading day (plan section 1).

- **The private dashboard (Hex).** Hex is a hosted notebook, a private wall of charts over the same database (plan section 1). It will show your watchlist by sector and country, value versus price, Monte Carlo ranges, the accuracy scoreboard, and a descriptive "is the market on the rails" panel built from Damodaran's own implied ERP series (ERP is the equity risk premium, the extra return investors demand for holding stocks rather than government bonds) (plan section 1). The scoreboard measures the bias (whether past valuations ran high or low on average) and the variance (how widely they scattered) of past valuations against realized prices (plan section 1). You look at it; you do not print it (plan section 1).

- **Cursor as your daily tool.** Cursor is the editor you already pay for; the system's tools plug into it as an MCP server, a standard socket that lets an editor call outside tools (plan section 1). You will type requests such as "value Trent" or "write the memo for Apple" (plan section 1). Cline, which is free, sits in the same window as a bench for trying open-weight models (models whose files are published, so anyone can run them) (plan section 1). Think of Cursor as the dashboard of a car whose engine lives elsewhere.
