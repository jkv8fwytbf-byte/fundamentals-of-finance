## How to read the tables

A few terms first.

- Value per share is what his model said one share was worth. Price is what the market charged that day.
- The gap between the two is his claim. When value sits below price, he is saying the market is paying too much.
- A stock split divides each old share into several new ones. Prices before a split look huge next to prices after it. Each section notes the split so you compare like with like.
- Market cap is the price of one share times the number of shares. It is the price tag on the whole company.
- TAM means total addressable market, the total sales a company could ever win. It drives most of the disagreements below.
- An IPO is the first sale of shares to the public. The IPO price is what the bankers set.

The tables also record his own trades. Those are historical facts about one man's account, and he reports them himself. They are not guidance for yours.

## What he got right

### The 2020 series

Between 26 Feb 2020 and 5 Nov 2020 he wrote fourteen "Viral Market" posts. All fourteen are in the local corpus under blog/2020/. He kept valuing companies through the crash. He published the implied equity risk premium as it moved; it reached 6.01% on 1 Apr 2020. He refused to switch to pricing multiples (ratios such as PE, price divided by earnings per share). He bought during the drawdown. On 16 Mar 2020 he wrote that "Value on the other hand has both upper and lower bounds", while prices have none. On 24 Apr 2020 he published data showing the market was punishing low-PE, high-dividend stocks more than high-PE non-payers. His words: "disappointing news for value purists". He printed evidence against his own camp in the hardest month available.

### WeWork, 2019

On 17 Sep 2019 he wrote about how new listings inflate their TAM. Uber's filing implied a TAM of $5.71 trillion across 175 countries, and WeWork claimed $3 trillion of office-space opportunity. On 15 Nov 2019 he analyzed SoftBank's rescue as a sunk cost problem. A sunk cost is money already spent that should not sway a new decision. The post records SoftBank writing down its WeWork stake by $4.6 billion, from a $47 billion pricing to about $8 billion with the new money. He called the private price wrong before the collapse, and the collapse came.

## The pattern, in three lines

::: {.reading-emphasis .key-idea}
- He is usually right about the short run and wrong about the decade. Facebook, Amazon and Tesla share this shape.
- His errors are story errors, not arithmetic errors. Tesla's factory, Uber's market and Blinkit's worth were all narrative calls.
- He publishes every value before the outcome, then publishes the scorecard, including the losses.

Your engine inherits the first two weaknesses. From M1 onward it will store every value with its date and its vintage ids, so that you can grade it later the way he grades himself. Chapter 6 turns that into design rules.
